On 8 October 2026, the studio I work for had 18 first messages drafted to small businesses, each pointing out something missing from their website. Before any went out, I built the free fix each message offered. That meant opening the inner pages. In 9 of the 18 drafts, a claim was wrong: four said "no contact form" when the form was on the Contact page, three said "no map" when the map was on the Contact page, and two said "not on HTTPS" about sites that had a valid certificate but didn't forward visitors to it. The audit behind the drafts had only read each home page.

We rewrote all nine and ran the same audit again on 200 small-business websites, this time reading up to eight pages each: the home page plus whatever looked like contact, booking, location or about pages. Of the 113 sites with a Google Maps link or embed, 83 had it only on an inner page. Of the 116 with a contact form, 64 had it only on an inner page. A home-page-only check would have made 147 false "missing" claims about a map or a form.

This guide is for anyone about to write to a business about its website: a freelancer, a small web agency, someone starting out offering website fixes. It's how to check a site properly before you say anything, and how to word what you found so it stays true.

Why the home page lies

Small-business sites put the practical things on the Contact page. In our 200, the most common places the map turned up were /contact/, /contact-us/ and /contact. Forms were the same. The home page has the photos and the pitch; the "how to reach us" lives one click away, in the menu.

The owner knows this. They built the Contact page. If your message says "there's no way to contact you from your website", they read the first line, know it's false, and stop reading. Every true thing you said after that is lost with it.

"Not found" isn't proof either. Our crawl read the page's HTML as the server sent it. A booking widget or form that a script draws after the page loads can be invisible to that kind of check and plain to see in a browser. The 84 sites in the "no form" segment above are where we didn't find one, not a count of sites without one.

Step 1: list the pages worth reading

Open the home page and read the menu, including the footer. Note every link whose words mean any of these: Contact, Contact us, Book, Booking, Appointments, Reserve, Find us, Location, Visit, About. Open each one.

Then look at the sitemap, which lists the site's pages. Try /sitemap.xml on the domain (WordPress sites often use /wp-sitemap.xml, and many shops and builders publish one at /sitemap.xml that links to others). Search it for the same words. Pages that aren't in the menu sometimes are in the sitemap.

On 130 of our 200 sites there were between one and three of them besides the home page. Five minutes is enough.

Step 2: check each claim on each page

For each page, open the page source: right-click, then View Page Source (Ctrl+U on Windows, Cmd+Option+U in Chrome on a Mac). Press Ctrl+F (Cmd+F) and search.

  • A map: search for maps. A Google Maps embed or link shows up as google.com/maps, maps.app.goo.gl or goo.gl/maps. Then click it. On one of our sites, the map on the Contact page pointed to a different area from the address written next to it. That's a real finding, and a more useful one than "no map".
  • WhatsApp: search for wa.me and whatsapp. A floating green button is usually one of these.
  • A contact form: search for <form, then read what's inside. A search box is a form; it isn't a contact form. A box that only takes an email is a newsletter signup. A contact form has a message box or at least two fields for the visitor to fill in. Our checker counted a form only when it had a message box (<textarea) or two or more fields, and skipped search forms.
  • Online booking: look for a "Book" button on the page itself, not just in the source. Booking is often a link out to a separate booking service, so follow it.

If the source has nothing but the page in your browser shows a form or a booking calendar, trust the browser. A script built it after the page loaded.

Step 3: HTTPS is three different findings

"Your site isn't secure" covers three separate problems with three different fixes. In our 200: 5 sites had no working HTTPS at all, 35 had a valid certificate but let visitors stay on the plain http:// address, and 11 still listed http:// addresses in their sitemap or robots.txt. Only the first group is "not on HTTPS".

Don't test this in the browser's address bar. Chrome tries https:// first when you type an address, so you see the secure version even when the site itself never sends anyone there. Use curl, which comes with macOS and Windows 10 and later. On Windows, type curl.exe in PowerShell (plain curl there is a different command).

First, does the secure address work?

curl -sS -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code} %{url_effective}\n" https://example.com/

On Windows PowerShell:

curl.exe -sS -o NUL -w "%{http_code} %{url_effective}\n" https://example.com/

A status like 200 https://example.com/ means the certificate is fine. A line like curl: (60) SSL certificate problem: certificate has expired means it isn't, and that's the real "not secure" finding.

Second, does the plain address forward to the secure one?

curl -sL -o /dev/null -w "%{http_code} %{url_effective}\n" http://example.com/

(Windows: curl.exe and -o NUL again.) The -L follows redirects, and the address printed is where you end up. If it starts with https://, the site forwards. If it still starts with http://, as it does for example.com when I ran it on 8 October, the site answers on both and doesn't send anyone to the secure one. The true sentence is "typing your address without https keeps visitors on the unsecured version", not "your site isn't on HTTPS".

Third, the leftovers:

curl -s https://example.com/robots.txt | grep -i sitemap

(Windows: curl.exe -s https://example.com/robots.txt | Select-String -Pattern sitemap.) If the sitemap line starts with http://, so do the addresses search engines are told about. That's a small finding. Say it as a small finding.

Step 4: say what you checked

Write each finding with its scope. Compare:

  • "Your website has no contact form." True only if you read every page, and a reader can't tell whether you did.
  • "Your home page has no contact form; the one on your Contact page is two clicks from the menu." True, specific, and it shows you looked.
  • "I checked your home page, Contact and About pages and didn't find a map." True, and it leaves room for the page you missed.

The second and third versions are weaker sales lines. They are also the only ones that survive the owner clicking their own menu.

Step 5: keep the evidence next to the message

For each claim, write down the page address you checked and what you saw there, before you send. Ours goes through a review step that reads the message and its evidence together. The first message we tried to send was refused, because no evidence was attached to it. Attaching the page-by-page check fixed it, and every message since carries one.

You don't need a review step to do this. A note under each draft with the URLs you opened is enough. When the owner replies "we do have a form", you can answer with the page you read, or admit you missed one. Either is better than guessing.

Step 6: build the fix before you offer it

This is what caught our nine. To write a fix, you have to look at the page the fix goes on. Adding a map to a Contact page that already has one is impossible to plan without noticing it. If you offer a free fix in your message, make it before you send, not after they say yes.

What not to do

  • Don't trust a scanner that reads one page. Ask which pages it read. If it doesn't say, assume the home page.
  • Don't count a search box or a newsletter signup as a contact form, either way.
  • Don't report "not secure" from the browser's address bar.
  • Don't list ten findings. Pick the two you have checked best. One false finding in ten makes the other nine look guessed.

The short version

  1. Open the menu and footer links for Contact, Book, Location and About, and search the sitemap for them.
  2. On each page, search the source for maps, wa.me and <form, and look at the page in a browser too.
  3. Test HTTPS with curl: does https:// work, does http:// forward to it, does the sitemap still list http://?
  4. Write each finding with the pages you checked.
  5. Keep the URLs you read next to the message, and build the fix before you send.

On 200 sites, the home page alone would have been wrong about the map on 83 of them and about the contact form on 64. The Contact page is one click. Click it.
